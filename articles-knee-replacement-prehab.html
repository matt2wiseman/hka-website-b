Prehab before a knee replacement: why the weeks before surgery matter

Got a total knee replacement booked? The strength and movement you build beforehand can make the first weeks after surgery easier. Here's what prehab involves and how to get started.

MWMatt Wiseman
Physiotherapist and Accredited Exercise Physiologist · [Month Year]

Key points

  • Prehab is a structured exercise and education program in the weeks before surgery.
  • Stronger thigh muscles before surgery are linked with better function afterwards.
  • Aim for 4–8 weeks, with 2–3 supervised or home sessions a week.
  • Prepare your home and your expectations, not just your knee.

What is prehab?

Prehab, short for prehabilitation, means getting your body ready for surgery. It usually combines strengthening, range-of-motion work, general fitness and education about what to expect, all tailored to how your knee is now.

Why bother if the knee is being replaced anyway?

A new joint replaces the worn surfaces, but it doesn't rebuild the muscles that control it. Years of knee pain often leave the quadriceps (the big muscles at the front of the thigh) weaker, and strength drops further in the first weeks after surgery. Starting from a higher baseline gives you more in reserve.

Research backs this up. People with stronger quadriceps before surgery tend to function better a year later.1 Reviews of prehab programs show improvements in pain, strength and function before surgery, with some benefit in the early weeks afterwards.2 Longer-term effects are less clear, but the downside is small and the habits you build carry straight into your rehab.

What prehab looks like

It starts with an assessment of your strength, range of motion, walking and balance, plus what you want to get back to. From there, a typical program runs 4–8 weeks and includes:

  • Strength: sit-to-stands, step-ups, leg press or wall squats, bridges, hip and calf work.
  • Range of motion: getting the knee as straight as possible matters as much as bending it.
  • Fitness: a stationary bike or pool sessions to keep your heart and lungs conditioned.
  • Practice runs: using crutches or a frame, getting in and out of bed and the car, and managing stairs.

Some discomfort while exercising is normal with an arthritic knee. We'll set a level that's acceptable for you and check that it settles within 24 hours.

Three exercises to start with

  1. Sit-to-stand. From a firm chair, stand up and sit back down slowly without using your hands if you can. 3 sets of 8–10.
  2. Straight leg raise. Lying on your back with the other knee bent, tighten your thigh and lift the straight leg to the height of the bent knee. 3 sets of 10.
  3. Knee straightening. Sit with your heel resting on a rolled towel and let the knee relax towards straight for 2–5 minutes.

If your knee is very swollen or these increase your pain for more than a day, stop and get assessed first.

Get your home ready

  • Clear trip hazards like loose rugs and cords.
  • Choose a firm chair with armrests, high enough that your hips sit above your knees.
  • If stairs are hard, set up a downstairs base with everyday items at waist height.
  • Stock the freezer and line up help and transport for the first couple of weeks. You won't be driving until your surgeon clears you.

Can prehab be funded?

If you have knee osteoarthritis, your GP may refer you under a Medicare chronic condition management plan. Most private health extras policies cover physiotherapy and many cover exercise physiology. Veterans can use their DVA card with a referral.
